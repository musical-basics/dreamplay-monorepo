# AB Test August 10: what our first buyers told us

**Sent** 2026-08-10 to 62 buyers · **Analyzed** 2026-08-14 · 26 responses (17 surveys, 9 call requests)

This is the first time we have asked our buyers why they bought. The answers
change what we should say in our marketing, what we should build next, and
where we are most fragile.

---

## 1. The headline findings

**Our buyers are not who a keyboard company would assume.** They are older,
mostly returning to an instrument they abandoned, and 15 of 18 physically
cannot reach an octave comfortably. They did not shop for a keyboard. They
shopped for a way back to playing.

**Nearly a third bought to support the mission, not to receive a product.**
Five of 18 named "I believe in the mission" as their single biggest reason,
above features or price. One founded a narrow-key advocacy society in Japan.
One is 80 and battling cancer, hoping to finally play his dream pieces. This
is a movement as much as a market, and it should be talked to that way.

**Price is doing more work than the survey admits.** Nobody selected price as
what almost stopped them, but that is survivorship bias: this cohort bought at
a median of **$550**, and half paid under it. They are the people for whom the
price was already low enough. The survey cannot tell us about everyone who saw
$899 and left. Buyer language treats affordability as the deciding factor
against Athena ("a steal", "the cheapest narrow-key option available"). See
§7.

**Waiting is the strain we cannot message away.** The most common hesitation
after "no hesitation" was *doubts that a new company could deliver* (5 of 18),
which is not a perception problem we can write our way out of. These buyers
ordered between December and July and the product does not exist yet. Their
doubt is an accurate reading of reality, and it resolves when units ship,
which needs capital, not copy. See §6.

**The Athena comparison is the competitive battleground.** Five buyers
mentioned NarrowKeys Athena unprompted. We win on price (roughly a third of
theirs) and on DS6.0 sizing. Three Athena owners told us 5.5 was too narrow
for them, and one plans to sell his Athena once ours arrives.

## 2. Method beats money: what the 2x2 actually proved

Four equal groups of ~16 buyers, split deterministically:

| Arm | Offer | Assigned | Responded | Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | Survey + $5 store credit | 16 | 10 | **63%** |
| A2 | Survey, no offer | 16 | 7 | 44% |
| B1 | Call + $10 store credit | 17 | 4 | 24% |
| B2 | Call, no offer | 15 | 5 | 33% |
| | **Survey arms** | 32 | 17 | **53%** |
| | **Call arms** | 32 | 9 | 28% |

### The survey wins on volume, roughly 2:1

53% versus 28%. A two-minute form asked of a stranger converts about twice as
well as a request for a live conversation. If the goal is breadth, survey.

### But calls produce far richer data per person

The nine call-arm responses became **8 call requests and 4 confirmed
conversations**. A single call yields more than any survey answer: Paul
Huang's request alone produced a detailed hardware critique (1/4" outputs,
shell size, gigging weight) that no multiple-choice question would have
surfaced. **Use surveys for breadth, calls for depth.** They are different
instruments, not competitors.

### Did the $5 help? Yes, but not how we expected

| | A1 (paid $5) | A2 (unpaid) |
|---|---|---|
| Response rate | 63% | 44% |
| "Why did you order", median length | 218 chars | 266 chars |
| **Answered the optional final question** | **80%** (8/10) | **29%** (2/7) |
| Total words written, median | 602 chars | 321 chars |

Three honest readings:

1. **The volume lift is promising but not proven.** 10 vs 7 out of 16 is a
   19-point gap, but at this sample size roughly a 1-in-4 chance of arising
   from noise. Treat as "worth repeating", not "settled".
2. **The strongest signal is the optional question**: 80% versus 29%. The $5
   buys *goodwill and effort*, not merely a click.
3. **It did not deepen the core answer.** Paid respondents wrote slightly
   *shorter* answers to "why did you order". The unpaid group self-selected
   for people motivated enough to write unprompted. The incentive widened the
   funnel; it did not make people more thoughtful.

**Cost: $55 in store credit across 11 grants.** For three extra responses and
substantially more optional detail, that is cheap. Keep the $5 on surveys.

### The incentive did nothing for calls

B2 (no offer, 33%) slightly outperformed B1 (with $10, 24%). Whatever makes
someone agree to talk to a founder, ten dollars is not it. Do not pay for
calls; ask well instead.

### Email performance was flat across all four arms

Open rates 56-67%, click rates 33-63%, with no meaningful separation. The
subject lines were not the variable that mattered. What people were asked to
*do* determined the outcome.

## 3. Who our buyers are

**Age** skews old: 45-59 is the largest group (6), 60+ next (4), 18-29 (5),
30-44 (2). This is not a youth product.

**Experience**: "Returning after years away" dominates (8 of 18), then
intermediate (4), professional/teacher (2), advanced (2), beginner (2). We are
selling to people who *stopped playing* and want back in.

**Hand span is the qualifying fact**: 8 play "with some strain", 7 "barely"
reach an octave, only 3 "easily". Fifteen of eighteen are in physical
difficulty on a standard keyboard.

**Almost entirely for themselves**: 17 of 18. Only one teacher bought for a
student. Gifting and parent-buying is essentially absent from this cohort.

**Discovery is founder-led**: Lionel's YouTube channel (7) leads, then social
media (4), friend/family/teacher (3), Google (2), other YouTube (1). The
channel is the top of the funnel; word of mouth is real but small.

## 4. Why they bought, in their words

**Primary reason, counted:**

| Reason | n |
|---|---|
| Narrow keys that finally fit my hands | 10 |
| I believe in the mission and wanted to support it | 5 |
| It is for someone with smaller hands | 1 |
| Relief from pain or strain | 1 |
| Other | 1 |

### Theme A: reaching what was always out of reach

The single most repeated concrete desire is **the tenth**. Not "comfort",
not "ergonomics", but a specific interval they have never been able to play.

> "The fact that I would now be able to reach 10ths to play cool chords; and
> to help prevent future injury's from strain." (Max, 18-29, intermediate)

> "I have always wanted to play certain piano pieces properly, i.e. the way
> they should sound if I could reach all of the stretches. 'Wedding Day At
> Troldhaugen' by Grieg comes to mind." (Jon Robin, 80, teacher)

**Marketing implication:** name the repertoire. "Play the tenth" is a
sharper promise than "keys that fit your hands".

### Theme B: pain, injury, and giving up

> "As I have gotten older, I have developed more pain in the fingers, wrists
> and elbows... It allowed me to go back to a regular practice schedule
> without a return of the pain." (Paul, 60+, advanced jazz)

> "I grew up playing the piano and thought I'd eventually grow into the size
> of the keys. Unfortunately that didn't happen, and I eventually stopped
> playing after highschool." (Alina, 18-29)

One Japanese buyer injured her right thumb playing Liebestraum and Chopin's
Op. 10 No. 3, and abandoned performance entirely.

**Marketing implication:** the emotional core is not convenience, it is
*quitting*. Many of these people stopped playing. We are selling return.

### Theme C: the mission, and trust in the founder

> "Seeing Lionel's passion behind the project, and knowing I was helping not
> only myself, but supporting thousands of other pianists with similar hand
> size issues as me." (Brian, 45-59)

> "There is no good reason why piano playing should be gate-keeped by those
> with physical advantages. Options for all should exist." (Collin, 45-59)

> "I could sense your enthusiasm for this project and wanted to support it."
> (Kurumi, 45-59)

**Marketing implication:** the founder story is not a nice-to-have, it is a
primary purchase driver. Five people bought the mission first and the
keyboard second.

### Theme D: price against the Athena

> "The next best option that exists on the market today is the NarrowKeys
> Athena, which costs at least $1,500 for even the most basic version, and
> $2,500 for the pro version. A narrow-key piano that cost only $600 for a
> founders' edition was a steal." (Joe, 30-44)

> "It was the cheapest narrow-key option available on the market. I think the
> only other option was the Athena, which was three times the price."
> (Josef, 18-29)

**Marketing implication:** we are the affordable entry to narrow keys. That
positioning is already working; make it explicit.

### Theme E: DS6.0 is a genuine differentiator

Three Athena owners independently said **5.5 was too narrow for them**:

> "I tried that and found it was too narrow and I was constantly hitting 9ths
> instead of octaves." (Ryan, 30-44)

> "I found the 5.5 distance to be just too small for me... when playing at
> higher speeds, my fingers would just naturally reach for wider spans."
> (Paul, 60+)

> "I had Athena but 5.5 was too narrow." (Dan, 60+)

**Product implication:** DS6.0 is not a compromise size, it is a competitive
weapon aimed directly at Athena's dissatisfied owners. Our order book already
reflects this: 35 DS5.5 versus 19 DS6.0, but DS6.0's share has climbed from
27% of early orders to 56% since April.

## 5. What they are asking us for, unprompted

1. **Pro in normal colors.** Two buyers independently said Nightmare Black and
   Aztec Gold are too showy: *"I like the features of the Pro, but the look of
   the two non-pro options."* We added Midnight Black (Standard Keys) as a Pro
   finish on 2026-08-06; this validates it. Consider a plain white Pro too.

2. **Gigging hardware** (Paul, professional): 1/4" L/R outputs rather than a
   3.5mm jack, and a smaller shell. *"Despite the narrower dimensions of the
   keybed, it is still placed in a conventional 88 key shell, so size and
   weight are about the same as a regular 88 key piano, which is disappointing
   for a gigging musician."* A narrow 88 in a full-width case forfeits a real
   advantage.

3. **Repair assurance on first builds** (Michal): *"If there are problems with
   the DreamPlay over time, on these initial builds, it would be really nice
   that there is a way to get it repaired without an extra cost."*

4. **Sizing guidance.** One beginner is still undecided between 5.5 and 6.0
   and worries about feeling crowded between black keys. We have no sizing
   tool. Several buyers are guessing.

## 6. Where we are fragile, and what is actually in our control

The single root cause behind most of the anxiety in these responses is that
**we have not delivered yet**. Buyers have been waiting since as early as
December 2025. Everything else is downstream of that.

It is worth separating the two kinds of fragility, because only one of them
is a thing we can fix this week.

### Capital-bound: not fixable by messaging

The delay itself. No amount of better copy, faster replies or friendlier
tone shortens the gap between order and delivery. That gap closes when
tooling is funded and production runs, which means **more sales or an
investor**. Until then, "doubts that a new company could deliver" is not a
misunderstanding to be corrected: it is a correct assessment that we ask
buyers to hold in suspension on trust.

> "AI suggested I do not [upgrade], since the whole DreamPlay design is in
> its initial development without any case history and it is terribly late.
> So at this point it is just trust." (Michal, 60+)

That is a customer reasoning her way toward leaving, in writing, and choosing
to stay anyway. We should read it as a warning about the clock, not as a
copywriting failure.

**The honest framing: trust is not our bottleneck, it is our runway.** It
holds while people keep hearing from us. It is finite, and it is spent by
time.

### Operationally-bound: entirely our fault, and fixable

Delay sets the baseline anxiety. **Our own misses are what convert patient
waiting into anger.** Two concrete instances in the last month:

1. **Kurumi Marron** requested the Pro upgrade on 16 April, was told on 26
   April to expect instructions "within a week", and heard nothing. When she
   used the August configuration link, the page told her she was *"not
   eligible for the Pro upgrade"*: a bug where saving the upgrade set a Pro
   finish, which then made her look ineligible (fixed 2026-08-06). She wrote:
   *"I simply cannot accept being abruptly told that I am not eligible... I
   expect a sincere response."* **Support has since resolved this.** She is
   also the most influential buyer we have: she recruits other buyers to us
   from a Japanese advocacy society. We nearly lost her to a form bug, not to
   the delay.

2. **Zoom links for four confirmed founder calls were never generated** until
   2026-08-14, hours before the first call. A buyer who had already said yes
   had no way to join.

Neither of these needed capital. Both compounded the delay's damage, because
a buyer who is already waiting reads every silence as evidence.

**Priority: eliminate operational misses.** They are cheap to fix and they
are what actually loses people.

### The blind spot

36 of 62 buyers responded to nothing at all. We know a great deal about the
vocal 42% and almost nothing about the majority. Silence is not necessarily
contentment.

## 7. These findings are investor material

The most valuable thing in this dataset may not be marketing guidance. It is
**documented, named evidence of unmet demand**, gathered from people who paid
for a product that does not exist yet:

- **15 of 18 respondents physically cannot comfortably reach an octave** on a
  standard keyboard. 7 "barely", 8 "with some strain".
- **Buyers quit playing over this.** Multiple respondents stopped entirely:
  one after high school, one after injuring her thumb on Liebestraum and
  Chopin Op. 10 No. 3.
- **The alternative costs 3x.** Buyers independently cite NarrowKeys Athena at
  $1,500 basic and $2,500 pro against our ~$600 founders' price.
- **Athena owners are defecting to us over sizing.** Three separate owners
  said 5.5 was too narrow for them; one intends to sell his Athena once ours
  arrives. DS6.0 is an identified, underserved segment inside an already
  underserved market.
- **Organised advocacy exists.** A buyer founded the Japan NarrowKey Piano
  Society, commissioned custom KLUGE narrow keybeds, lends instruments to
  Niigata University, earned national press coverage, and actively recruits
  buyers to DreamPlay.
- **People bought the mission before the product.** 5 of 18 named belief in
  the mission as their primary purchase reason, ahead of any feature.
- **Price is a real constraint, and our order book shows it.** Zero
  respondents *named* price as their blocker, but they are the people who
  already found the price acceptable: median paid **$550**, and buyer language
  is explicitly comparative ("a steal", "three times the price"). Demand is
  proven at $500-600. Demand at $899 is unproven. See §7a.

This is a market thesis with names, quotes and payment records attached, not
a hypothesis. Anyone assessing whether narrow-key demand is real can read
Appendix B and decide for themselves.

## 7a. Price: what our own order book proves

The survey says price stopped nobody. **Our order book says price is the
single best-evidenced thing about our demand.** Both are true, and the second
matters more, because the survey only ever interviewed people who already
said yes.

### What buyers actually paid

66 real buyers, all amounts as paid (deposits included):

| | |
|---|---|
| Median paid | **$550** |
| Mean paid | $602 |
| Under $550 | **33 buyers (50%)** |
| $550-700 | 28 buyers (42%) |
| $700+ | 5 buyers (8%) |
| Range | $299 to $2,029 |

Monthly medians have never left the mid-hundreds: $499 (Dec), $549 (Jan),
$569 (Feb), $613 (Mar), $553 (Apr), $524 (May), $507 (Jun), $532 (Jul).

**Eighteen of 66 bought a 50% reservation**: more than a quarter of our buyers
chose to split the payment rather than pay in full, which is itself a price
signal.

**Read that against today's pricing.** Buyers who joined at $499-$650 are the
entire evidence base for demand. Demand at $899, and at the $1,899 Pro, is
**not evidenced by anything in this dataset**. Two people bought Pro-tier
products; both were existing buyers upgrading, not new customers arriving at
that price.

### The survey's blind spot, stated plainly

Nobody named price as their hesitation because **everyone who found the price
too high never became a buyer and never received the survey**. The correct
conclusion is not "price does not matter". It is "price was acceptable to the
people for whom it was acceptable". Our 0-purchase site funnel (249 sessions,
63 configurations, 13 begin_checkout, zero conversions) is where the price
objection would actually show up, and it is exactly where we are failing.

### Where the buyer language points

Affordability is the argument buyers use when they explain the decision to
themselves:

> "A narrow-key piano that cost only $600 for a founders' edition was a
> steal in comparison." (Joe)

> "It was the cheapest narrow-key option available on the market. I think the
> only other option was the Athena, which was three times the price."
> (Josef)

> "It was a better price than the Athena." (Samantha)

> "The price was also reasonable." (Brian)

Four buyers volunteered price as the reason *in prose* while none selected it
in the multiple choice. That gap is the finding.

### The DreamPlay Go hypothesis (Lionel, 2026-08-14)

A **$600 DreamPlay Go with 128-note polyphony** sits precisely at the price
our order book already validates, and would rebuild the entry point that
originally created this buyer base.

**What the data supports:**

- $600 is at the top of the band where 92% of our buyers actually transacted.
- Half our buyers paid under $550, so a $600 model is not a discount play, it
  is a return to the real market price.
- The Athena comparison works only while we are dramatically cheaper. At $899
  we are "less expensive"; at $600 we are "a third of the price", which is
  the argument buyers repeat unprompted.
- Beginners and returners dominate this cohort (8 returning, 2 just
  starting). 128-note polyphony is not a constraint any of them named. Nobody
  in 18 responses asked for more polyphony.

**What to be careful about:**

- **Do not strand existing buyers.** Anyone who paid $549-$650 for a Piano
  Bundle must not discover that a later, cheaper model is comparable to
  theirs. Position Go as *below* the One on specification, not beside it, and
  tell existing buyers what they still get that Go does not.
- **The narrow keys must not be the thing that is cut.** Narrow keys are the
  entire product thesis (10 of 18 bought for them). Go must ship DS5.5 and
  DS6.0, or it is a different company's product.
- **Protect the professional segment.** Paul (gigging jazz) needs 1/4"
  outputs and a lighter shell. Those are Pro or One features; do not let Go
  cannibalize the tier that funds development.
- **A third tier adds decision cost.** Buyers are already unsure between 5.5
  and 6.0, and two asked for less flashy Pro finishes. Three models with two
  sizes and multiple finishes is a lot of choosing for a category most
  visitors have never encountered. Sizing guidance becomes mandatory, not
  optional.

**What would confirm it before tooling money is spent:** we have a site that
converts nobody at current prices and a live A/B framework. A Go landing page
at $600, tested against the current offer, would produce a real answer for
the cost of a test rather than a production run. If a $600 narrow-key
keyboard cannot convert traffic, the problem is not price and we will have
learned that cheaply.

## 9. What to do with this

**Marketing**
- Lead with **the tenth**, not with ergonomics. Name repertoire.
- Tell the **return story**: these buyers quit, and we are how they come back.
- Keep the **founder story** central. It is a primary purchase driver, not decoration.
- Say the **Athena comparison out loud**: same idea, a third of the price.
- Market **DS6.0 to Athena owners** who find 5.5 too narrow. That is a
  precise, reachable, underserved audience.

**Product**
- Ship a plain-finish Pro. Two requests unprompted from a base this size is a signal.
- Investigate a **narrower shell** for the 88 (real advantage currently forfeited).
- Consider **1/4" L/R outputs** for the professional segment.
- Publish a **repair/warranty commitment** for the first production run.
- Build a **sizing guide**: hand-span to DS5.5/6.0/6.5, since buyers are guessing.

**Research process**
- Surveys for breadth, calls for depth. Run both.
- Keep the **$5 on surveys**; drop the incentive for calls, it does not work.
- Follow up survey responders with call invitations, referencing what they
  wrote. They already gave time; the second ask is easier than the first.
- **Chase the 36 silent buyers**, ideally with a one-question version. Our
  biggest blind spot is the majority who said nothing.

## 10. Brief for a marketing analyst: fixing website conversion

**The problem to solve.** Our site gets traffic and captures almost nobody.
Over 249 scored A/B sessions (4-12 Aug, admin and bot traffic excluded):
**63 product configurations, 13 begin_checkout events, 0 purchases, and 4
email signups (1.6%)**. Six homepage variants have been running since 4
August and none has separated from the others (score spread 6.0-9.3 sits
inside noise at ~40 sessions per arm). Identical failure across every layout
means this is **a funnel and messaging problem, not a layout problem**.

**What this document gives you that analytics cannot.** Eighteen buyers who
actually paid, telling you in their own words why. Appendix B is the full
verbatim set. Read it before touching the site.

### The conversion gap, stated plainly

The people in Appendix B all crossed a line that visitors will not cross:
they paid several hundred dollars for a keyboard that does not exist, from a
company with no track record, with a delivery date a year out. If we can
learn what made *them* say yes, we can find the visitors who would say yes
too.

Their answers cluster on things our website mostly does not say.

### Five hypotheses worth testing, drawn from the buyer language

1. **Lead with the tenth, not with ergonomics.** Buyers do not describe the
   product as "comfortable" or "ergonomic". They name an interval they have
   never been able to play. *"Now be able to reach 10ths to play cool
   chords."* *"Wedding Day At Troldhaugen... a very awkward section which
   would be easy on a smaller keyboard."* Test a hero built on a concrete
   musical promise instead of an abstract benefit.

2. **Sell the return, not the upgrade.** The dominant experience group is
   "returning after years away" (8 of 18), and several **quit entirely** over
   hand size. *"I eventually stopped playing after highschool."* The site
   currently addresses people shopping for a keyboard. The buyer is more
   often someone who gave up. Test messaging aimed at return rather than
   acquisition.

3. **Qualify visitors by hand span, early.** 15 of 18 buyers cannot
   comfortably reach an octave, and buyers are **actively guessing** about
   sizing: one is still undecided between 5.5 and 6.0 after ordering; three
   Athena owners discovered 5.5 was too narrow only after buying. We ship no
   sizing tool. A hand-span measurement widget is both a qualification
   mechanism and, judging by variant 2a, our best-converting kind of lead
   magnet (see §9 note below).

4. **Put the Athena comparison on the page.** Five buyers made the comparison
   unprompted, and it is the argument that closed them: *"at least $1,500 for
   even the most basic version... $600 for a founders' edition was a steal."*
   Visitors are almost certainly making the same comparison silently, without
   our numbers in front of them. Test an explicit comparison.

5. **Test the price itself, not just the words around it.** Our buyers
   transacted at a median of $550 and the site now asks $899. Nobody has
   tested whether the current price converts, and a 0-purchase funnel is
   consistent with a price objection nobody is voicing. A $600 DreamPlay Go
   landing page (§7a) tested against the current offer would answer this for
   the cost of a test rather than a production run.

6. **Make the founder the funnel, because he already is.** Discovery is
   YouTube-led (7 of 18 from MusicalBasics), and 5 of 18 bought the *mission*
   ahead of the product. The site treats the founder story as an About page.
   For this audience it may be the conversion asset. Test founder-forward
   landing pages against product-forward ones.

### The one piece of internal evidence already pointing somewhere

Of 4 email signups across 249 sessions, **2 came from variant 2a, the only
variant whose hand-measuring guide is an inline, email-gated card** (a
converted Dropbox link, 5 Aug). Every other variant offers the Small Hands
Guide as "coming soon". Working hypothesis: **an instantly delivered asset
beats a promised future one.** The obvious next test is to put a gated,
instantly delivered hand guide on 5a (our engagement leader: most time on
page, 423 clicks, 13 CTA clicks) while keeping 2a as control.

### Questions the analyst should answer

- Where exactly do the 13 begin_checkout sessions die? Nobody has traced the
  drop between configuration and payment.
- Does delivery timing kill conversion at the point of sale? A January 2027
  ship date on a page has never been tested against alternative framings.
- Is the price presented as a bargain relative to Athena, or as an
  unexplained several-hundred-dollar ask? Buyers who paid describe it
  comparatively ("a third of the price"); the site states a number.
- **Does the current price convert at all?** Every buyer we have came in
  around $550. We have never tested $899 against a lower entry price on live
  traffic. This is the highest-value unanswered question on the list.
- Does the site ever ask a visitor about their hands? Buyers self-identify by
  a physical trait; the site sells a product category.
- What is the single next commitment we want from a visitor? At 1.6% email
  capture we may be asking for the purchase far too early.

## Appendix A: method

- 62 buyers, deterministic 4-way split (sha256 of a fixed salt + buyer id,
  chosen before send for an exact 16/16/16/16 balance).
- Rewards were **store credit** in an append-only ledger, one grant per
  reward type per buyer.
- Open tracking via pixel, click tracking via URL parameters (no redirects,
  after redirect-mode collapsed Gmail deliverability on 2026-05-03).
- Small samples throughout. The A1/A2 gap is suggestive, not conclusive;
  the survey-versus-call gap (53% vs 28%) is large enough to act on.
- Live dashboards: `/admin/buyers/research` (results),
  `/admin/ab-test-august-10` (editor).
